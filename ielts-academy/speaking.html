Module 05 · Speaking

Speaking: Fluent, Natural Band 7

The Speaking test is a real conversation with an examiner, not a performance. Band 7 means you can talk at length on familiar and abstract topics without noticeable effort, with some natural idiomatic language and mostly accurate grammar.

💡
Accent is not the problem

You do not need a British or American accent. Examiners judge how easy you are to understand. A clear Vietnamese accent with correct final sounds and word stress can score 7–8.

5.2Part 1: interview

Short, natural answers about yourself. The formula is simple: Answer → Reason → Detail. Don't give one-word answers, and don't give a speech.

Answer

Respond directly, with a natural opener: Not really, to be honest… / Yes, quite a lot actually… / It depends…

Reason

Why? …mainly because… / The thing is…

Detail

An example, contrast with the past, or a personal detail. For instance… / When I was younger…

Band 7+ sample answers

Q: Do you prefer paper books or e-books?
Honestly, I'd say paper books, although I read more on my phone these days simply because it's convenient. There's something about holding a real book that helps me concentrate — I don't get distracted by notifications, which is a real problem when I read on a screen.
Q: Has your hometown changed much in recent years?
Yes, enormously. When I was a kid, Da Nang was a fairly quiet coastal city, but over the last decade it's become a major tourist destination. There are high-rise hotels all along the beach now, and the traffic is much heavier than it used to be — although, to be fair, the infrastructure has improved a lot as well.
Q: Are you a morning person or an evening person?
Definitely an evening person. I'm much more productive later in the day — that's when I do most of my studying. Mornings are a bit of a struggle for me unless I've had a strong cup of coffee.
⚠️
Avoid
  • Memorised answers — examiners recognise them and they hurt Fluency.
  • Repeating the question word for word: “My hometown has changed much in recent years.” Paraphrase or just answer.
  • Starting every answer with “I think”. Vary: I'd say / Personally / To be honest / Well, actually.

5.3Part 1 step by step: every question type

Part 1 questions are quick, but each type has a natural shape. Recognise the type in the first second, and the answer almost builds itself. Target: 2–4 sentences, 15–25 seconds.

  1. Catch the tense and the key word1 s

    Do you… (present habit) · Did you… as a child (past) · Would you like… (hypothetical) · Has it changed (present perfect). Answering in the right tense is an easy grammar point.

  2. Answer directly3 s

    Start with a natural opener, not a repeated question: Yes, quite a lot actually… / Not really, to be honest… / I'd say… / It depends…

  3. Add a reason5–8 s

    …mainly because… / The thing is… / That's probably because…

  4. Extend once5–10 s

    Choose one extender: an example, a past vs now contrast, an exception (unless… / except when…), a feeling, or a future plan. Then stop and let the examiner move on.

One topic, every type: worked answers

Preference · Do you prefer cooking at home or eating out?
I'd go for cooking at home, to be honest. Eating out is fine for special occasions, but it's far more expensive, whereas at home I can control exactly what goes into my food. What I enjoy most is trying new recipes at the weekend.
Past · Did you help with cooking when you were a child?
Yes, quite a lot. My grandmother would let me help her make spring rolls before Tet, and I remember being really proud of my slightly messy ones. Back then it felt like a game, whereas now I realise she was teaching me a real skill.
Change · Have your eating habits changed in recent years?
They have, actually. Since I started working, I've been eating out at lunchtime much more, mainly because there's no time to cook. I'm trying to cut down, though, because it's taking a toll on my budget.
Hypothetical · Would you like to take a cooking course?
I'd love to, if I ever had the time. I'd probably choose something like Italian cooking, since it's so different from Vietnamese food — as long as the course isn't too expensive.
💡
Don't over-extend

Part 1 is not Part 3. A 45-second answer to “Do you like fruit?” sounds rehearsed and eats into the examiner's time. Short, natural and accurate wins.

5.4Part 2: long turn

The examiner gives you a cue card, paper and a pencil. You have 1 minute to prepare, then speak for up to 2 minutes. Band 7 candidates keep talking until the examiner stops them.

Describe a skill you learned that took a long time.

You should say:

  • what the skill was
  • when and how you learned it
  • what difficulties you had

and explain how you feel about this skill now.

1-minute notes (keywords only)

driving · 3 yrs ago · after uni new job far · motorbike in rain 😩 driving school, 2×/week, patient instructor difficulties: motorbike habits, width, parking ×10, failed test – stalled on slope extra lessons, Dad, weekends → passed 2nd feel now: proud – being bad at first = normal

Write a few words per bullet, plus one story and one feeling. Stories create fluency; feelings give you language for the “explain” point.

Band 8 sample answer (~2 minutes)

~255 words spoken

I'd like to talk about learning to drive, which, to be honest, took me far longer than I expected.

I started taking lessons about three years ago, just after I graduated, because my new job was on the other side of the city and commuting by motorbike in the rainy season was becoming exhausting. I signed up with a driving school near my house and had two lessons a week with an instructor who was incredibly patient — which was lucky, because I needed a lot of patience from him.

The main difficulty was that I had been riding a motorbike for years, so I had all sorts of habits that simply don't work in a car. I kept forgetting how wide the car was, and the first time I tried to park between two other cars, I think it took me about ten attempts. On top of that, I failed the practical test the first time because I panicked on a slope and stalled the engine. That was pretty discouraging, and for a few weeks I seriously considered giving up.

But I booked a few extra lessons, practised on quiet roads at the weekend with my father, and passed on my second attempt.

Looking back, I'm actually quite proud of it — not because driving is a remarkable skill, but because it taught me that being bad at something at first is completely normal. Now I drive to work most days and I can even park in one go… well, most of the time.

If you run out of things to say

Add: a comparison (“Compared with learning to swim…”), a consequence (“Because of that, I now…”), a future plan (“Next, I'd like to…”) or another person's reaction (“My father thought…”). Never stop at 1:20 and wait.

5.5Part 2 step by step: the 1-minute plan

Two minutes feels long only when you don't know where your talk is going. This system turns the 60 seconds of preparation into a route you can follow under pressure.

  1. Read the card and name its type5 s

    Person · place · object · event / experience · activity or skill · media (book, film, website) · ideal / future. The type tells you the tense: a past experience → past tenses; “a place you would like to visit” → would and conditionals.

  2. Choose your story fast10 s

    Pick the option you have the most details about, not the most impressive one. It can be real, adapted or invented; examiners assess language, not truth. Don't change your mind after this point.

  3. Fill the note grid40 s

    Keywords only, 2–4 per box: Setting (when / where / who) · Story (start → problem → outcome) · Details (names, numbers, senses) · Feelings (then vs now) · Explain (two reasons for the last bullet).

  4. Fix your first and last lines5 s

    First: I'd like to talk about…, which… Last: a reflection: Looking back, … / If I had the chance, I'd… Knowing both ends removes the panic.

The 2-minute route

0:00Intro: what it is + one hook
0:10Background: the first bullets (when, where, who)
0:40The story: what happened, a problem or turning point, vivid details
1:20“Explain…”: two reasons, developed
1:50Reflection

If you reach your reflection before the examiner stops you, use an extender: a comparison, a consequence, someone else's reaction or a future plan.

♻️
One story, many cards

Prepare 6–8 rich personal stories (a trip, a difficult challenge, a celebration, a teacher, a skill, a gift). With small changes, each one can answer many cards: the driving story fits “a skill”, “a challenge”, “a time you were proud”, “something that took a long time”.

Part 2 planner

Get a random cue card, plan with the note grid during the 60-second countdown, then speak for two minutes while the timer runs.

5.6Part 3: discussion

Part 3 decides whether you are a 6 or a 7. Questions move from personal to general and abstract: society, comparisons, causes, the future. Use this framework:

1. Direct answer

Generally speaking, I don't think…

2. Reason

The main reason is that…

3. Example

In Vietnam, for instance…

4. Balance

That said… / In the long run…

Band 7+ sample answers

Q: Do you think schools teach practical skills well enough?
Generally speaking, I don't think they do — at least not in Vietnam. The curriculum is still quite exam-focused, so students spend most of their time memorising information rather than learning things like managing money or communicating effectively. That said, things are starting to change: some schools now run life-skills programmes, and I'd like to see those become compulsory, because in the long run those skills probably matter more than being able to recall formulas.
Q: Will online learning replace classroom teachers?
I doubt it will replace them completely. Online courses are great for flexibility — you can learn at your own pace — but they rely heavily on self-discipline, which many students, especially younger ones, simply don't have yet. What I think is more likely is a kind of hybrid model, where teachers act more as guides and use technology to support what they do in class.
Q: Are people less patient than in the past?
I'd say they probably are, and technology is a big part of the reason. We've become used to getting everything instantly — food delivered in twenty minutes, answers to any question in seconds — so waiting feels far more frustrating than it did for our parents' generation. Having said that, I'm not sure it's entirely negative; it has also pushed businesses to become much more efficient.

5.7Part 3 step by step: every question type

Part 3 tests whether you can discuss ideas, not just describe your life. Each question type has its own logic. Identify it, apply the framework, and aim for 4–6 sentences (30–60 seconds).

  1. Identify the question type2 s

    Opinion? Comparison? Cause? Effect? Solution? Prediction? Hypothetical? Use a natural buying-time phrase while you decide: That's an interesting question… / Well, it depends…

  2. Give a direct position5 s

    One clear sentence that answers the question. Hedge where appropriate: Generally speaking… / On the whole, I'd say…

  3. Explain the reasoning10–20 s

    Why? How does it work? Use complex sentences: …because…, which means that…

  4. Support with a general example10–15 s

    Speak about people in general or your country, not only yourself: In Vietnam, for instance, many young people…

  5. Balance or look ahead5–10 s

    An exception, the other side, or the future: That said… / Of course, this isn't true for… / In the long run…

“Do you think…? · Is it important…? · Do you agree that…?”

Position I'm fairly convinced that… / I'd agree up to a point. Reason The main reason is that…, which means… Example You can see this in… Balance That said, it's not always the case, especially when…
Is it important for children to learn to cook?
I'd say it's more important than most people realise. Cooking isn't just a practical skill: it teaches children about nutrition, which means they're less likely to rely on fast food when they leave home. In Vietnam, for instance, a lot of students who move to the city for university end up eating instant noodles every day simply because they never learned. That said, I don't think it needs to be a school subject; parents are probably in a better position to teach it.

“How is… different from the past? · Do young and old people… differently? · City vs countryside?”

Position There's a huge / fairly small difference. Side A In the past / Older people tend to… Side B whereas these days / younger people are far more likely to… Why This is largely down to…
How has the way people shop changed compared with the past?
It's changed dramatically, at least in big cities. A generation ago, people would go to the local market almost every day and buy fresh food from stallholders they knew personally, whereas these days a lot of shopping happens on apps like Shopee. This is largely down to convenience: people work longer hours and simply don't have time. Having said that, traditional markets are still very much alive in smaller towns.

“Why do people…? · What makes…? · What are the reasons for…?”

Main cause I think the main reason is… Explain Because…, people tend to… 2nd cause On top of that, … / Another factor is… Example For example, …
Why do many young people want to work for large companies?
I think the main reason is security. Big companies usually offer stable contracts and benefits like health insurance, which matters a lot when you're just starting out and paying rent for the first time. On top of that, having a well-known name on your CV can open doors later in your career. Mind you, I've noticed that more of my friends are interested in start-ups now, because they want more responsibility early on.

“How does… affect…? · What impact has… had on…?”

What are the advantages and disadvantages of living in a big city?
The obvious advantage is opportunity: there are far more jobs, universities and services, so young people can build a career much faster. The flip side, though, is that life is expensive and stressful; in Ho Chi Minh City, many people spend over an hour in traffic every day. On balance, I think cities make sense when you're young, but a lot of people move somewhere quieter once they have a family.

“What can be done about…? · Should the government…? · Whose responsibility is it…?”

What could be done to reduce traffic in cities?
I think the responsibility lies mainly with local governments. One thing they could do is make public transport genuinely convenient; Hanoi's new metro lines are a good start, but people need frequent buses to reach the stations. If getting to work by train were faster than driving, people would switch without being forced to. Of course, it wouldn't solve everything: individuals also need to change their habits.

“How will… change in the future? · Do you think… will…?”

Will people still read printed books in the future?
I imagine they will, although probably fewer of them. We're already seeing e-books and audiobooks take a large share of the market, especially among commuters. If this carries on, printed books might become more of a luxury item, something people buy as gifts. Then again, I doubt they'll ever disappear completely, because a lot of readers simply enjoy the feel of paper.

“What would happen if…? · If you could…, what would you…?”

What would happen if everyone worked from home?
If that happened, I think cities would change completely. Traffic and pollution would fall dramatically, which would then mean that people could live further from city centres. The people who would suffer most are probably small businesses like cafés and restaurants that depend on office workers. Realistically, though, many jobs, such as nursing or construction, can never be done from home.

Collocation beats rare words

The descriptor rewards less common and idiomatic vocabulary used with awareness of collocation. A heavy workload, a tight budget, a strong accent score better than an obscure word used slightly wrongly.

5.9Pronunciation for Vietnamese speakers

Vietnamese is a tonal, mostly single-syllable language with very few final consonant clusters. These differences create predictable problems — and fixing just the first three usually raises Pronunciation by half a band.

ProblemExampleHow to fix
1. Dropped final consonantslike → lie, five → fi, rice → ryeExaggerate final sounds when practising; record yourself and listen only for endings.
2. Missing -s and -ed endingsHe work… / I walk yesterday-s: /s/ (cats), /z/ (dogs), /ɪz/ (buses). -ed: /t/ (walked), /d/ (played), /ɪd/ (wanted).
3. Word stresse-co-NO-my instead of e-CO-no-myLearn stress with every new word (dictionary: ˈ mark). Stressed syllables are longer and louder.
4. /θ/ and /ð/think → tink / sink; they → dayTongue between the teeth and blow air.
5. /ʃ/ vs /s/ship ↔ sip, she ↔ see/ʃ/: round your lips, tongue further back.
6. Consonant clustersstreet → sơ-tri, months, textsDon't insert a vowel between consonants; practise slowly, then speed up.
7. Flat or “choppy” rhythmEvery syllable equally strongStress content words (nouns, verbs) and reduce function words (a, to, of): “I WANT to GO to the SHOP”.
8. No linkingturn / it / offLink consonant to vowel: tur_ni_toff, an_apple.

  • I'd say… / Personally, I feel…
  • I'm fairly convinced that…
  • As far as I'm concerned…
  • I'm in two minds about it.

Speculating & predicting

  • It's quite likely that… / I doubt…
  • I imagine that in the future…
  • There's a good chance that…
  • If things carry on like this, …

Past vs present

  • When I was growing up, … whereas these days…
  • People used to…, but now they tend to…
  • Compared with a generation ago, …
  • Things have changed dramatically since…

Paraphrasing a word you don't know

  • It's a kind of… / It's a sort of…
  • It's what you use to…
  • I can't remember the exact word, but it's…
  • The person who… — I think it's called…

Idiomatic language (use sparingly)

  • It's not my cup of tea. — I don't like it
  • I was over the moon. — very happy
  • It costs an arm and a leg. — very expensive
  • I'm snowed under at work. — very busy
⚠️
Idioms: quality over quantity

One natural idiom used correctly helps; five forced idioms (“It's raining cats and dogs”) sound memorised and can lower your score. Natural collocations (a tight budget, a heavy workload, a strong cup of coffee) matter far more.
