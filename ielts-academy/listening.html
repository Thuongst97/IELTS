Module 3 · Listening

Listening: 30/40 and Above

Band 7 in Listening means about 30 correct answers out of 40. You don't need to understand every word — you need to predict what's coming, follow the speaker's signposts, avoid distractors and spell perfectly.

3.1Format & what Band 7 needs

Listening is the same for Academic and General Training. You hear four recordings, each played once, with time to read the questions before each one. Difficulty rises from Part 1 to Part 4.

PartSituationTypical tasksBand 7 aim
Part 1 · Q1–10Everyday conversation, 2 speakers (booking, enquiry, registration)Form / note / table completion9–10 / 10
Part 2 · Q11–20Everyday monologue (tour, radio talk, induction)Multiple choice, map/plan labelling, matching8 / 10
Part 3 · Q21–30Academic discussion, 2–4 speakers (students + tutor)Multiple choice, matching opinions6–7 / 10
Part 4 · Q31–40Academic lecture, 1 speaker, no breakNote / summary / sentence completion6–7 / 10

💻 Computer-delivered

Type answers as you listen. At the end you get only 2 minutes to check. You can highlight words and write notes on screen. Spelling is still marked strictly.

📝 Paper-based

Write on the question paper while listening, then you get 10 minutes to transfer answers to the answer sheet. Use CAPITAL LETTERS to avoid ambiguous handwriting.

🎯
The Band 7 maths

Most practice materials convert 30–31 correct = Band 7.0 and 32–34 = 7.5. The easiest marks are in Parts 1 and 2, so losing marks there to spelling or plurals is the most common reason students stay at 6.5. Secure 17–18/20 in the first half and you only need ~13/20 in the harder second half.

3.2Question types & tactics

Every question type has a reliable method. Use the reading time before each part to predict the type of answer (a number? a noun? a place?) and underline the keywords you will listen for.

Method

  1. Read the word limit first. “ONE WORD AND/OR A NUMBER” means 15 July is fine but the 15th of July breaks the limit only if counted as extra words — write the shortest correct form.
  2. Predict the grammar of each gap: after a/an → singular noun; after £ → number; after on → day or date.
  3. Answers come in order. If you miss one, move on immediately.
  4. Copy the exact words you hear. Don't paraphrase the answer.

What gets tested

  • Names spelled letter by letter (surnames, street names)
  • Phone numbers (“double seven”, “oh” for 0)
  • Dates, times, prices, postcodes
  • Corrections: “Tuesday — sorry, Thursday”
  • Plurals: if the speaker says parks, “park” is wrong

Method

  1. Read the question stem carefully; it tells you what to listen for. Underline who/what/why.
  2. Expect all three options to be mentioned. Two are rejected, delayed, or belong to someone else.
  3. Don't tick the first option you hear. Wait for the end of the speaker's turn.
  4. The correct option is almost always paraphrased; an option that repeats the speaker's exact words is often the trap.

Signals the answer is coming

What really struck me…Actually…The main reason…In fact…But the biggest…My advice would be…

Signals an option is being rejected

I wouldn't do thatThat was the plan, but…no surprise thereused toI'm not sure

Method

  1. Find where you start (“You are here”, the entrance) and the orientation (north arrow, top/bottom of the map).
  2. Name the fixed landmarks in your head: path, fountain, lake, car park.
  3. Follow the speaker with your pen/finger. Directions are relative to the walker, not to you — “on your right” when walking north = east on the map.
  4. Questions are in order of the talk, which usually follows a route.

Direction language

  • opposite / facing / across from
  • just beyond / past / before you reach
  • at the far end / in the corner / on the edge
  • to the north-west of / alongside / adjacent to
  • take the second turning / bear left / go straight ahead

Method

  1. The list of items (1, 2, 3…) is in the order you'll hear them; the box of options (A–G) is not.
  2. Spend reading time paraphrasing each option in your own words: “use only the statistics” → just the figures / the numbers.
  3. Listen for the decision in Part 3, not the first opinion: speakers often disagree before agreeing.
  4. Some options are not used. Some may be used twice (the instructions will say).

Part 3 warning

With three speakers, keep track of who says what. A question like “What is Maya's opinion…?” is a trap if Leo says the opposite first.

Method (Part 4)

  1. Part 4 has no break: read all ten questions before it starts.
  2. Use headings in the notes as a map of the lecture: when the lecturer says “Secondly…” or “Now, there are some challenges”, move to that heading.
  3. Answers are usually nouns — often academic words you must spell (maintenance, environment, government, accommodation).
  4. Check the grammar fits: “reduce the risk of ____” needs a noun or -ing form.

Lecture signposts

Let's begin with…The first reason is…On the other hand…The drawback is…Finally…Despite this…In the next lecture…

3.3Distractors & paraphrase traps

The test is designed so that the wrong answers are also spoken. Learn these five patterns and you will stop losing “easy” marks.

TrapWhat you hearCorrect answer
Self-correction“The class is on Tuesday… oh no, sorry, it's moved to Thursday.”Thursday
Price / time change“It used to be £32, but it's now £34.”34
Rejected suggestion“We thought about leaving it out — I wouldn't do that.”Not “leave it out”
Not yet true“We're hoping to build a play area next year.”Not a recent change
Wrong speakerLeo: “I thought they were interesting.” Maya: “We could have left them out.”Maya → not useful
Number distractor“A dark roof reaches 70 °C. A green roof can be up to 40 degrees cooler.”40

Paraphrase: the skill behind every question

Questions rarely use the speaker's words. Train yourself to hear meaning, not matching words:

Question saysVolunteers are needed to repair paths through the trees.
Speaker saysThe woodland paths need clearing and repairing.
Question saysVisitors should not give food to birds.
Speaker saysPlease don't feed the ducks and swans.
Question saysThe presentation should end with suggestions for local government.
Speaker saysFinish with concrete recommendations for the local council.
🇻🇳
Watch out — common for Vietnamese learners
  • Final sounds: Vietnamese words rarely end in clusters, so the ear “drops” endings. fourteen/forty, cost/costs, walk/walked sound the same until you train for them.
  • Plural -s: write exactly what you hear. insects ≠ insect.
  • Spelling of common words: accommodation, environment, government, necessary, Wednesday, February, restaurant, temperature.
  • Letters that sound alike: A/E/I, G/J, B/V/P, M/N. Drill them in the trainer below.

3.6Band 7 training routine

Listening improves through volume plus precision: lots of natural English, and short daily sessions of intensive, word-by-word work.

DAILY · 15 MINIntensive dictationTake 2 minutes of a BBC 6 Minute English episode. Write every word, replay sentences, then check the transcript. Note every missed ending.
DAILY · 10 MINShadowingPlay a sentence, pause, repeat it with the same rhythm. Trains your ear for linking (“turn_it_off”) and weak forms.
DAILY · 20 MINExtensive listeningTED talks, podcasts, documentaries — on Part 4-style academic topics: environment, history, psychology, technology.
WEEKLY · 40 MINOne full test + reviewA Cambridge IELTS test under exam conditions, then 30 minutes analysing every wrong answer with the transcript.
