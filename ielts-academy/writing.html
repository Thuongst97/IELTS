Writing: Task 1 & Task 2 at Band 7

Writing is the skill where most candidates get stuck at 6.0–6.5. The difference between 6 and 7 is not fancy vocabulary — it is a clear position, fully developed ideas, controlled complex grammar and far fewer errors.

4.1Scoring criteria: Band 6 vs 7 vs 8

Each task is marked on four criteria, each worth 25%. Your Writing band is a weighted average in which Task 2 counts twice as much as Task 1. The summaries below paraphrase the public band descriptors — focus on the Band 7 column: that is your checklist.

TA / TR

Task Achievement (T1) / Task Response (T2): did you answer the question fully?

CC

Coherence & Cohesion: logical organisation, paragraphing, linking.

LR

Lexical Resource: range, precision, collocation, spelling.

GRA

Grammatical Range & Accuracy: complex structures, error-free sentences.

Band 6
  • All parts addressed, but some more fully than others
  • A relevant position, but conclusions may be unclear or repetitive
  • Main ideas relevant, but some underdeveloped or unclear
Band 7 ✦ target
  • All parts of the question addressed
  • A clear position throughout the essay
  • Main ideas extended and supported (explanation + example)
  • May over-generalise or lose focus slightly
Band 8
  • All parts sufficiently addressed
  • Well-developed response with relevant, extended, well-supported ideas
  • Only occasional lapses
Band 6
  • Overview present, information appropriately selected
  • Key features adequately covered, but some details irrelevant or inaccurate
Band 7 ✦ target
  • Covers the requirements of the task
  • A clear overview of main trends, differences or stages
  • Key features clearly presented and highlighted (could be more fully extended)
Band 8
  • All requirements covered sufficiently
  • Key features clearly presented, highlighted and illustrated with well-chosen data
Band 6
  • Coherent arrangement, overall progression
  • Linking used, but sometimes faulty or mechanical
  • Referencing not always clear; paragraphing not always logical
Band 7 ✦ target
  • Information logically organised, clear progression
  • A range of cohesive devices, incl. reference and substitution, used flexibly (some over/under-use)
  • Each paragraph has a clear central topic
Band 8
  • Logical sequencing throughout
  • All aspects of cohesion managed well
  • Paragraphing sufficient and appropriate
Band 6
  • Adequate range for the task
  • Attempts less common vocabulary, with some inaccuracy
  • Some spelling/word-form errors that don't impede meaning
Band 7 ✦ target
  • Enough range for flexibility and precision
  • Uses less common items with awareness of style and collocation
  • Only occasional errors in word choice, spelling or word formation
Band 8
  • Wide range used fluently and flexibly
  • Skilful use of uncommon items; rare errors
Band 6
  • Mix of simple and complex sentence forms
  • Some errors in grammar and punctuation, rarely reducing communication
Band 7 ✦ target
  • A variety of complex structures
  • Frequent error-free sentences
  • Good control of grammar and punctuation; a few errors
Band 8
  • Wide range of structures
  • The majority of sentences error-free
  • Only very occasional errors
⚠️
Automatic penalties
  • Under length (fewer than 150 / 250 words) lowers TA/TR. Aim for 170–190 and 270–300.
  • Off-topic or partly answered questions cap Task Response — answering only one half of a two-part question can hold you at 5.
  • Memorised template phrases are not counted and may be penalised. Write naturally.
  • No overview in Task 1 usually limits TA to Band 5.

4.2Task 1 Academic: reports

You describe a visual — a graph, chart, table, diagram of a process, or maps — in at least 150 words. You report what you see; no opinions, no causes that aren't shown.

The four-paragraph structure

1. Introduction Paraphrase the task in one sentence: The [chart type] compares/shows [what] in [where] [when]. 2. Overview 2 sentences on the BIG picture — main trends, biggest/smallest, overall change. No numbers. ← the most important paragraph for Band 7 3. Body 1 Group the data logically (e.g. the rising lines / the first period / the largest categories). Support with figures. 4. Body 2 The remaining group, with comparisons. Every number should be connected to a comparison or a trend.

4.3Task 1 step by step: every visual type

Band 7 in Task 1 is mostly decided before you write: finding the overview and grouping the data. Use the same 20-minute routine for every visual, then apply the type-specific plan below.

  1. Decode the visual1 min

    Read the title, axes, units and time frame. Note four things in the margin: what is measured, unit (%, thousands, tonnes), where, when. The time frame sets your tense: past years → past simple; projections → is expected / predicted to; no time → present simple.

  2. Find the big picture2 min

    Ask the four overview questions: What is the overall direction? Which is the largest / smallest? What changed most? Is there a pattern most items share, or a clear exception? Choose two answers. They become your overview, with no numbers.

  3. Group the data1 min

    Decide the logic for your two body paragraphs: by trend (risers vs fallers), by size (major vs minor categories), by time (first half vs second half) or by change type (added vs removed on maps). Each paragraph should carry similar weight.

  4. Select the evidence1 min

    Circle 3–4 figures per paragraph: start and end points, peaks and lows, crossover points and the exception. Leave out everything else; selection is part of the score.

  5. Write13 min

    Introduction (1 sentence, paraphrased) → overview (2 sentences) → body 1 (3–4 sentences) → body 2 (3–4 sentences). Every number must sit inside a trend or a comparison; never list figures.

  6. Check2 min

    Tense, number / amount / proportion, prepositions (rose to 40%, by 10%, from … to …, peaked at), articles, and 170–190 words.

Plans by visual type

Ask yourself

  • Which lines rose, fell or stayed flat overall?
  • Which line ended highest, and was it also highest at the start?
  • Do any lines cross? When did one overtake another?
  • Is there a period of sudden change, a peak or a plateau?

Group by

Similar trends (the two rising lines vs the falling one), or two time periods when all lines change direction at the same point.

Intro The line graph illustrates [what] in [where] between [year] and [year]. Overview Overall, [A and B] saw an upward trend, whereas [C] declined. The most striking feature is that [A], initially the second-highest, ended the period well ahead of the others. Body 1 The rising lines: start → key change → end, with one comparison. Body 2 The falling or stable line: start → end, plus the crossover point.
Band 7 frames
  • X rose steadily from 12,000 in 2000 to 19,000 in 2010, before climbing sharply to a peak of 46,000.
  • X overtook Y in around 2013 and remained the highest thereafter.
  • After a period of stability at around 6,000, the figure for Z tripled within five years.
  • By the end of the period, X and Y had converged at 21,000.

⚠ Trap: describing every year in order. Describe the shape (start, turning point, end) instead.

Ask yourself

  • Is the x-axis time? If so, treat it like a line graph (trends).
  • If it shows categories: which is highest / lowest, and by how much?
  • With two years or two groups, where is the gap biggest and where did it narrow?
  • Is there one category that behaves differently from the rest?

Group by

Largest categories vs smallest, categories that increased vs decreased, or group A vs group B (e.g. men vs women).

Intro The bar chart compares [what] across [categories] in [years]. Overview Overall, participation rose in every age group, and the young remained the most active. The gap between the youngest and oldest groups narrowed considerably. Body 1 The leading categories, with exact figures and one comparison. Body 2 The categories with the biggest change, or the exception.
Band 7 frames
  • The proportion was highest among 18–29-year-olds, at 61%, almost double the figure for the over-60s.
  • The most significant increase was seen in the 60+ group, where the figure nearly doubled.
  • In both years, X exceeded Y; however, the gap narrowed from 32 to 23 percentage points.

⚠ Trap: going bar by bar. Compare across bars in every sentence.

Ask yourself

  • Which segment is the largest in each pie? Did that change?
  • Which share grew or shrank the most between the pies?
  • Can small segments be grouped (“the remaining categories each accounted for under 10%”)?

Group by

Segments that grew vs segments that shrank, or the major shares vs the minor ones.

Intro The pie charts show how [total] was divided among [categories] in [year] and [year]. Overview Overall, [X] remained the largest category, while the share of [Y] changed most dramatically. Body 1 The largest shares and how they changed. Body 2 The growing / shrinking minor shares; group the very small ones.
Band 7 frames
  • X accounted for the largest share (40%), followed by Y at 25%.
  • The share of X halved, from 30% to 15%, whereas Y more than doubled.
  • X made up roughly a quarter of the total, a proportion that remained almost unchanged.

⚠ Trap: “rose by 5%” when you mean 5 percentage points (30% → 35%).

Ask yourself

  • Which row or column has the highest and lowest values?
  • Is there a row that leads on every measure, or an exception?
  • Do the rows fall into natural groups (high / middle / low)?
  • If there are years, what is the overall direction?

Group by

Similar rows (the three most expensive cities vs the rest), or the main measure vs the secondary measure.

Intro The table provides data on [measures] for [rows] in [year]. Overview Overall, [X] recorded the highest figures on most measures, while [Y] had the lowest. A notable exception was [Z]. Body 1 The leading group, with the extremes. Body 2 The remaining group and the exception.
Band 7 frames
  • Figures ranged from 3.2 million in X to 8.1 million in Y.
  • X recorded the highest figure on every measure except Z.
  • At the other end of the scale, Y attracted just over a third as many visitors.

⚠ Trap: copying every cell. Choose the extremes and the pattern.

Ask yourself

  • What is the main point of each visual?
  • How are they connected? Does one explain the other?
  • Which single feature would you mention if you had one sentence?

Group by

One body paragraph per visual. The overview must cover both.

Intro The bar chart shows [A], while the table gives information about [B]. Overview One sentence for each visual's main feature. Body 1 Visual 1: key features with figures. Body 2 Visual 2: key features, linked back: “This is reflected in…”

⚠ Trap: an overview that mentions only one visual. That caps Task Achievement.

Ask yourself

  • How many stages are there? Where does it start and end?
  • Is it a cycle (natural) or a linear man-made process?
  • Where is the natural midpoint to split it into two paragraphs?
  • What do you need to turn into verbs? (collection → is collected)

Group by

First half of the stages vs second half; or preparation vs production vs distribution.

Intro The diagram illustrates how [product] is produced / how [thing] works. Overview Overall, the process consists of [N] main stages, beginning with [first] and ending with [last]. (It is a continuous cycle / a linear process.) Body 1 Stages 1 to midpoint, in order, mainly present passive. Body 2 Remaining stages to the end product.
Band 7 frames
  • At the first stage, used glass is collected from bottle banks and transported to a recycling plant.
  • Once it has been sorted by colour, the glass is crushed, after which it is melted at 1,500°C.
  • The molten glass is then poured into moulds in order to form new bottles, which are finally delivered to shops.

⚠ Trap: copying labels word for word and repeating “then”. Vary sequencers: following this, subsequently, once…, after which, at the final stage.

Ask yourself

  • What is the overall change: more developed, more residential, less green?
  • What was removed, added, converted or extended?
  • What stayed the same?
  • Is the second map a plan (future tense)?

Group by

Area (north / south of the river) or type of change (removed vs added). Use compass directions consistently.

Intro The maps illustrate the changes that took place in [place] between [year] and [year]. Overview Overall, the area became far more [developed / residential], with most of the farmland being replaced, although [X] remained unchanged. Body 1 Changes in area 1 / removals. Body 2 Changes in area 2 / additions.
Band 7 frames
  • The farmland to the north of the river was cleared to make way for a housing estate.
  • The old factory was converted into a shopping centre, while the car park was extended.
  • A new bridge is planned to be built, linking the town centre with the university.

4.4Task 1 language bank

Upward trends

  • rose / increased / grew / climbed (verb)
  • a rise / an increase / growth (noun)
  • surged / soared (very fast)
  • doubled / tripled / quadrupled
  • peaked at / reached a peak of

Downward trends

  • fell / declined / dropped / decreased
  • a fall / a decline / a drop
  • plummeted / plunged (very fast)
  • halved
  • hit a low of / bottomed out at

Stability & change

  • remained stable / unchanged / constant
  • levelled off / plateaued at
  • fluctuated around / between
  • overtook / surpassed
  • converged / was identical to

Degree & speed

  • sharply / dramatically / significantly
  • steadily / gradually / moderately
  • slightly / marginally
  • a sharp rise ↔ rose sharply
  • Use adjective + noun to vary sentence structure

Comparison

  • X was twice as high as Y
  • X was considerably higher than Y
  • the highest / the lowest figure was…
  • whereas / while / by contrast / in comparison
  • a similar pattern was seen in…

Approximation & proportion

  • approximately / roughly / just over / nearly
  • a quarter / a third / half / the vast majority
  • accounted for 40% of / made up
  • one in five people
  • the proportion / percentage / number (+ of)
💡
Number vs amount vs proportion

The number of people (countable) · the amount of water (uncountable) · the proportion/percentage of people (for %). Never write “the percentage of people rose by 20” — write by 20 percentage points or to 20%.

4.5Task 1 model answer

Task 1 (practice data). The graph below shows the average number of daily journeys made by bicycle in three cities between 2000 and 2025. Summarise the information by selecting and reporting the main features, and make comparisons where relevant. Write at least 150 words.
Band 8 level · 171 words

The line graph compares the average number of bicycle journeys made each day in three cities, Aldwick, Brenton and Corley, over a 25-year period starting in 2000.

Overall, cycling became considerably more popular in Aldwick and Corley, whereas it declined steadily in Brenton. The most striking change was in Aldwick, which went from the second-highest figure to by far the highest.

In 2000, Brenton recorded the largest number of daily bicycle journeys, at 30,000, compared with 12,000 in Aldwick and only 5,000 in Corley. Over the following 25 years, the figure for Brenton fell gradually, dropping to 21,000 by 2025.

Aldwick, by contrast, saw continuous growth. After a modest rise to 19,000 in 2010, the number of journeys climbed sharply, overtaking Brenton's figure around 2013 and reaching a peak of 46,000 in 2025 — almost four times the 2000 level. Corley's figure remained almost unchanged at around 6,000 for the first decade, before tripling to 18,000 in 2015. It then levelled off, ending the period at 21,000, exactly the same as Brenton.

Overview (TA)Cohesion (CC)Vocabulary (LR)Complex grammar (GRA)
🔎
Why this scores highly
  • The overview has no numbers and states the two big stories (two rising, one falling; Aldwick's dramatic change).
  • Data is grouped (Brenton alone, then the two risers), not listed year by year.
  • Every number is attached to a trend or comparison.
  • Varied sentence structures: participle clauses (overtaking…, reaching…), noun phrases (a modest rise).

4.6Task 2: essay types & structures

Spend 5 minutes planning: identify the essay type, underline every part of the question, decide your position, and choose two main ideas you can develop with an explanation and an example. Then write four or five paragraphs.

Recognise it: To what extent do you agree or disagree? · Do you agree or disagree?

Intro Paraphrase the statement + your clear position (agree / disagree / partly agree). Body 1 Main reason for your view: point → explain → example. Body 2 Second reason — OR — the opposing view and why it is weaker. Conclusion Restate your position and the main reasons. No new ideas.

Thesis examples: “While such schemes have clear benefits, I believe that making them compulsory would undermine their value.” · “I completely agree that…, for two main reasons.”

Recognise it: Discuss both views and give your own opinion.

Intro Introduce the debate + state your opinion (you must give one). Body 1 View A — why some people believe it (developed fairly). Body 2 View B — why others believe it. Usually the view you agree with. (Body 3) Optional short paragraph: your balanced position. Conclusion Summarise both views and confirm your opinion.

Recognise it: What are the causes? What are the problems? What solutions can you suggest?

Intro Paraphrase the situation + outline what the essay will cover. Body 1 Causes (2–3) or problems, each explained. Body 2 Solutions / effects — link each solution to a cause you mentioned. Conclusion Summarise the causes and the key solutions.

Recognise it: Do the advantages outweigh the disadvantages? · Is this a positive or negative development?

Intro Paraphrase + answer the question (e.g. “the advantages outweigh…”). Body 1 The side that is weaker in your view (concede it). Body 2 The side that is stronger — explain why it matters more. Conclusion Clear final judgement.

⚠️ “Outweigh” questions demand a judgement, not just two lists.

Recognise it: two different direct questions, e.g. Why is this happening? Is it a good thing?

Intro Paraphrase + brief answer to both questions. Body 1 Answer question 1 fully. Body 2 Answer question 2 fully. Conclusion Restate both answers.

⚠️ Answering only one question is the fastest way to a Band 5 in Task Response.

The developed body paragraph (PEEL)

Band 7 Task Response depends on extending and supporting ideas. Each body paragraph should follow one idea to its end:

Point

A topic sentence that states the paragraph's one main idea.

Explain

Why/how is it true? Develop the logic in 1–2 sentences.

Example

A specific, realistic example or consequence.

Link

Connect back to the question / your position.

4.7Task 2 step by step: brainstorm, argue, structure

Most Band 6 essays fail in the first five minutes: a half-answered question, a vague position or ideas that can't be developed. This routine makes the planning automatic.

  1. Decode the question2 min

    Underline the topic, the instruction (to what extent… / discuss both… / why… what…) and every limiting word (only, all, should, governments, in your country). Count the parts you must answer and name the essay type.

  2. Decide your position1 min

    Pick the side you can argue best, not necessarily the one you feel most strongly about. Write a one-sentence thesis now; it controls the whole essay. A qualified position (“agree, provided that…”) often reads as more mature.

  3. Brainstorm3 min

    Use the idea lenses below to list 4–6 rough ideas quickly. Don't judge them yet.

  4. Select and order1 min

    Keep the two ideas that answer the question most directly and that you can explain with a concrete example. Cut ideas that need specialist knowledge. Put your strongest idea first.

  5. Outline1 min

    Write the thesis plus one topic sentence per body paragraph, with 2–3 keywords for the explanation and the example.

  6. Write28 min

    Introduction 40–60 words · each body paragraph 90–110 words · conclusion 30–40 words. Target 270–300 in total.

  7. Check4 min

    Position consistent from intro to conclusion? Then scan for your personal error list: articles, plurals, subject–verb agreement, although… but, comma splices.

Brainstorming: five idea lenses

👥 Who is affected?

Individuals · families · employers / businesses · society · government · the environment. Ask: who gains, who loses? Each group can give you one idea.

🧭 Which area?

Economic · Social · Health · Education · Environment · Technology · Political. Run the topic through each and note anything that clicks.

⏳ Short vs long term

Immediate effect vs effect in 10–20 years. Strong Band 7 arguments often contrast the two: costly at first, but cheaper in the long run.

🔗 Why? → So what?

Take any idea and ask “why?” and “so what?” twice. Each answer adds a layer of development: cheaper housing → young people save → they start families earlier → birth-rate concerns ease.

⚖️ The other side

What would someone who disagrees say? This gives you a concession to acknowledge and answer, which shows a balanced, mature position.

🌏 Real context

Where have you seen this? Vietnam, your city, your school, a well-known company or policy. One realistic example beats three general claims.

Developing an argument: the idea ladder

Band 7 Task Response needs ideas that are extended and supported. Climb the ladder in every body paragraph:

1ClaimThe topic sentence: one clear idea that answers the question.
2ReasonThis is because… / The main reason is that…
3MechanismHow does it happen? …which means that… / As a result, …
4ExampleFor instance, in Vietnam… / A clear example of this is…
5LinkBack to your position: This shows why… / For this reason, …
Band 6 · listed, not developedWorking from home has many advantages. It saves time. It also saves money. Workers are happier. Therefore it is a good thing.
Band 7+ · one idea, fully developedThe most significant benefit of remote work is the time it gives back to employees. In large cities, commuting can easily take two hours a day, which means that workers often arrive at the office already tired. Without this journey, people can use the time to exercise, rest or spend time with their families. For example, many office staff in Ho Chi Minh City who switched to hybrid working report sleeping longer and feeling more focused. This suggests that the change benefits not only employees but also the companies that rely on their productivity.
💡
What counts as a good example?

A specific, realistic case: a country, a city, a type of company, a common situation (a student who works part-time…). Avoid precise invented statistics (“87% of people…”); they sound unconvincing, and a clear scenario is more persuasive.

Plans by essay type

Wording: To what extent do you agree or disagree? · Do you agree or disagree? You must give a clear extent of agreement and keep it consistent.

Position options

  • Completely agree / disagree: two reasons for your side.
  • Partly agree: one paragraph for each side, but say which side is stronger and why.
  • Agree with a condition: “…provided that / only if…”. Often the most convincing.

Brainstorm prompts

  • Why might the statement be true? For whom?
  • Where does it go too far (the word all, always, only)?
  • What would happen if the policy were introduced tomorrow?
Intro Paraphrase the statement. While it is true that…, I believe that… / I strongly agree that…, for two reasons. Body 1 Strongest reason: claim → reason → mechanism → example → link. Body 2 Second reason — OR — Admittedly, some argue that… However, this overlooks… Conclusion In conclusion, I (largely) agree/disagree that…, because… and…
Worked plan: “University education should be free for all students.”
Position: partly agree: free for lower-income students, not for everyone.
B1: free tuition widens access → talented students from poor rural families can study → social mobility (e.g. first-generation students in Vietnam).
B2: free for all is costly and regressive → wealthy families who could pay benefit most → money better spent on scholarships and schools.
Conclusion: means-tested free tuition achieves the goal more fairly.

⚠ Trap: “I agree” in the introduction and “there are both advantages and disadvantages” in the conclusion. A changing position caps Task Response.

Wording: Discuss both these views and give your own opinion. Three tasks: view A, view B, your view.

Position options

  • Agree with one view: present the other fairly first, then yours.
  • A middle position: both have merit in different situations; say which situation matters more.

Brainstorm prompts

  • Who holds view A, and what is their best argument?
  • Who holds view B, and what is their best argument?
  • Which argument is stronger, and why?
Intro Present the debate + your opinion (state it here). Body 1 Those who believe… argue that… (the view you agree with less, fairly developed) Body 2 On the other hand, others maintain that… (your view, more developed) Conclusion Although there is some merit in…, I believe that… because…
Worked plan: “Longer prison sentences vs other ways to reduce crime.”
Opinion: other ways are more effective.
B1 (longer sentences): deterrence + public safety while offenders are in prison; e.g. repeat violent offenders.
B2 (other ways): education and rehabilitation reduce reoffending; prisons can act as “schools of crime”; e.g. job-training schemes for prisoners.
Conclusion: long sentences for serious violent crime only; prevention elsewhere.

⚠ Trap: forgetting your own opinion, or giving it only in the last sentence.

Wording: Do the advantages outweigh the disadvantages? This is a judgement question: you must say which is greater.

Position options

  • Advantages outweigh: concede the disadvantages first, then the advantages.
  • Disadvantages outweigh: the reverse order.

Brainstorm prompts

  • List 2 advantages and 2 disadvantages using the “who is affected?” lens.
  • Which side is more serious, more permanent or affects more people? That is your reason for the judgement.
Intro Paraphrase + I believe the benefits of this trend outweigh its drawbacks. Body 1 The weaker side: Admittedly, this development has some drawbacks… Body 2 The stronger side + why it matters more: Nevertheless, these problems are outweighed by…, since… Conclusion Repeat the judgement and the deciding reason.
Worked plan: “More people work from home.”
Judgement: advantages outweigh.
B1 (drawbacks): isolation, blurred work–life boundaries; e.g. young employees miss informal learning.
B2 (benefits, greater): no commute → time and wellbeing; lower costs for firms; wider talent pool. Reason they outweigh: the drawbacks can be managed with hybrid schedules, while the benefits are structural.

⚠ Trap: two balanced lists with no verdict.

Wording: Is this a positive or negative development? Like “outweigh”: take a side, explain why.

Position options

  • Mainly positive / mainly negative.
  • Positive for one group, negative for another, with your overall verdict.

Brainstorm prompts

  • Who benefits from the trend, and who suffers?
  • Is the effect temporary or lasting?
Intro Describe the trend + In my view, this is a largely positive development. Body 1 Main positive effect, developed. Body 2 A second positive effect — OR — the negative side and why it is less significant. Conclusion Verdict + reason.

Wording: What problems does this cause? What solutions can you suggest? · What can be done?

Brainstorm prompts

  • Problems for individuals and for society (two levels).
  • For each problem, one solution: who should act (government, schools, companies, individuals)?
  • How exactly would the solution work?

The matching rule

Every solution should answer a problem you described. Examiners reward this logical link under Coherence and Task Response.

Intro Paraphrase + This essay will outline the main problems and suggest how they could be addressed. Body 1 Problem 1 + problem 2, each explained with an effect. Body 2 Solution to problem 1 (who + how) · solution to problem 2 (who + how). Conclusion Summarise problems and the key measures.
Worked plan: “People are living longer. Problems? Solutions?”
P1: pension costs → pressure on public finances and taxpayers. P2: health-care demand → hospitals overstretched.
S1: gradually raise the retirement age + encourage private saving. S2: invest in preventive care and community nursing so older people stay healthy at home.

Wording: What are the causes / reasons? What are the effects? · Why is this happening?

Brainstorm prompts

  • Causes: economic, social, technological, personal.
  • Effects: on individuals, on society, short-term vs long-term.
  • Use the “why → so what” chain to make each one a mini-argument.

Order

The most fundamental cause first. Effects can move from individual to society, or immediate to long-term.

Intro Paraphrase + outline: There are several reasons for this, and it has significant consequences. Body 1 The most fundamental cause is… A further factor is… Body 2 One major consequence of this is… In the long term, … Conclusion Summarise the causes and effects.

Wording: two direct questions, e.g. Why is this happening? Should old buildings be preserved? Answer both, with equal weight.

Brainstorm prompts

  • Write each question at the top of a column; brainstorm separately.
  • Q1 is usually factual (why / what): 2 reasons. Q2 is usually evaluative: your opinion + 2 reasons.

Introduction

Give a one-line answer to each question in the introduction. This shows the examiner your plan immediately.

Intro Paraphrase + brief answer to Q1 + brief answer to Q2. Body 1 Q1 fully answered (2 reasons, developed). Body 2 Q2 fully answered (your view + reasons). Conclusion Both answers restated.
Worked plan: “Why do people prefer to shop online? Is this positive or negative?”
Q1: convenience and wider choice; price comparison and home delivery.
Q2: mostly positive for consumers, but negative for small local shops and town centres → overall positive if local businesses also move online.

⚠ Trap: a long first answer and a two-line second answer. Both carry equal weight.

Some people believe that unpaid community service should be a compulsory part of high school programmes. To what extent do you agree or disagree?
Opinion · partly disagree · 293 words

It has been suggested that secondary school students should be required to do unpaid work in their local community as part of their studies. While I accept that such schemes can benefit both young people and society, I believe that making them compulsory would undermine much of their value.

There is no doubt that community service can be a valuable experience for teenagers. Working in a care home, a food bank or an environmental project exposes students to people and problems they would rarely encounter in a classroom, and this can foster empathy and a sense of civic responsibility. It can also develop practical skills, such as teamwork and time management, which employers increasingly value. For students who are unsure about their future careers, a placement may even provide a sense of direction.

However, the benefits of volunteering depend largely on the attitude of the volunteer. When students choose to help, they tend to be motivated and committed; when they are forced to, many will simply do the minimum required to obtain a certificate. Organisations such as charities may then find themselves supervising reluctant teenagers rather than receiving genuine help. Moreover, secondary school timetables are already crowded, and many students have family responsibilities or part-time jobs. Adding a compulsory requirement would place the greatest burden on those who have the least free time.

A more effective approach, in my view, would be for schools to encourage community work rather than require it. This could be done by building partnerships with local organisations, giving students credit for voluntary activities and celebrating their achievements publicly.

In conclusion, although community service offers clear educational and social benefits, I disagree that it should be compulsory, since obligation tends to weaken the very motivation that makes volunteering worthwhile.

Some people think that governments should spend money on improving public transport, while others believe that building more and better roads is more important. Discuss both views and give your own opinion.
Discussion · 293 words

Traffic congestion is a growing problem in many cities, and governments face a difficult choice about how to invest in transport. Some argue that the priority should be road building, while others believe that public transport deserves the greater share of funding. In my opinion, investment in public transport is the more sustainable solution, although roads cannot be neglected entirely.

Those who favour road construction point out that cars and lorries remain essential to modern life. In rural areas and smaller towns, public transport is often limited, so many people have no realistic alternative to driving. Goods are also transported mainly by road, which means that congested or poorly maintained roads can increase costs for businesses and, ultimately, for consumers. From this perspective, wider and better-maintained roads are vital for economic growth.

On the other hand, there is considerable evidence that building new roads does not solve congestion in the long term. Additional road space tends to encourage more people to drive, and within a few years the new roads become just as crowded as the old ones. Public transport, by contrast, can move far more people using the same amount of space: a single bus may replace dozens of cars. Efficient trains, trams and buses also reduce air pollution and carbon emissions, which are among the most serious problems facing cities today.

Having considered both sides, I believe that governments should direct most of their transport budget towards public transport in urban areas, while continuing to maintain existing roads and improving them where there is no alternative, such as in the countryside.

In conclusion, although roads will always play an important role, investing in public transport offers a more effective and environmentally responsible way to meet the transport needs of growing cities.

In many cities, young people find it increasingly difficult to afford housing. What are the causes of this problem, and what measures could be taken to solve it?
Causes & solutions · 279 words

In many cities around the world, housing has become so expensive that young people struggle to rent, let alone buy, a home of their own. This essay will examine the main reasons for this situation and suggest some measures that could help to address it.

The most fundamental cause is an imbalance between supply and demand. As employment and educational opportunities become concentrated in large cities, more and more people move there, but the construction of new homes has failed to keep pace. A second factor is that property is increasingly treated as an investment rather than simply a place to live. When investors buy apartments to rent out or to leave empty while their value rises, prices are pushed beyond the reach of first-time buyers. Finally, young people's incomes have not grown as quickly as housing costs, partly because many begin their careers on short-term or low-paid contracts.

Several measures could be taken to tackle this problem. Governments could increase the supply of affordable homes, either by building social housing directly or by requiring developers to include a proportion of lower-cost units in new projects. Taxes on empty or second homes would discourage speculation and encourage owners to make properties available for people to live in. In addition, improving transport links to suburbs and smaller towns would allow young people to live further from city centres without spending hours commuting each day.

In conclusion, the housing crisis facing young people is the result of insufficient supply, property speculation and stagnant incomes. Although there is no single solution, a combination of increased house building, tax reform and better transport could make housing considerably more affordable for the next generation.

Reference & substitution: the Band 7 cohesion skill

The descriptor rewards cohesion beyond linking words. Avoid repeating nouns by pointing back to them:

this / these + noun

Governments could tax plastic bags. This measure would… · These problems …

such + noun

…free tuition and student loans. Such schemes make education…

the former / the latter

Buses and trams… the latter are quieter but more expensive.

do so / one / ones

Many want to study abroad, but few can afford to do so. · old buildings and new ones

synonym chains

children → young people → the younger generation → students

paragraph bridges

Turning to the drawbacks, … · Equally significant is… · Despite these benefits, …
